The Challenge: An Extreme Test for a Mission-Critical System

1

Highly Engineering-Driven Initial Spec

At the beginning of the project, the client provided only a basic PPT spec filled with hardware data and feature lists, completely lacking consideration for the nurses' actual workflow. Developing directly from this would result in an unusable software in a clinical setting.
40

Beds Simultaneous Monitoring Limit

Nurses at a single station typically need to monitor around 40 beds simultaneously. In a highly stressful and busy environment, any redundant health charts or complex interfaces cause severe information overload, delaying the golden rescue time needed to "identify the problematic bed at a glance."
100%

Patient Individual Differences

Every patient's physical condition and mobility differ. Applying a one-size-fits-all "alert upon bed exit" standard would trigger a severe "cry wolf" effect (alarm fatigue) at the nursing station, ultimately causing medical staff to become numb to the alarms.

Strategic Decisions & Trade-offs

Weighing rough specs against real-world clinical scenarios

Product Definition: From 8 Original Items to 14 Core Tasks

Initially, the client provided a fragmented hardware presentation PPT listing about 8 thin features. I realized that if I simply acted as a "drawing machine," this system would never survive in a hospital.
I paused the visual work and proactively used task analysis to rigorously expand the client's original requirements into 14 core tasks. For example, noticing a lack of post-alarm follow-up mechanisms, I defined and added a "Remark system within the alert flow" to ensure information wasn't lost during nursing handovers. By mapping out all user tasks, we achieved perfect requirement alignment across teams before entering development.

Custom Parameters: Solving the "Cry Wolf" False Alarm Crisis

Once the task flow was established, the next threat was "alarm fatigue." I knew that applying a hospital-wide uniform alarm standard would lead to the system being muted at the nursing station.
I transformed the single alert condition from the PPT into an intuitive "Custom Parameter Panel." This allowed nurses to independently toggle detection items for individual patients and set precise timeframes and thresholds—such as triggering an alarm for "sitting on the bed edge for over 3 minutes," or setting an exclusive notification period for the night shift. Handing control back to the medical staff thoroughly filtered out invalid false alarms at the logical level.

Design Iterations & Micro-adjustments

Information simplification and colorology in extreme scenarios

Iteration 1: Homepage Simplification—From "Rainbow Pressure Maps" to "3 Life-Saving Data Points"

Information Disaster Caused by Following Specs
Initially, I completely followed the client's PPT proposal, stuffing "rainbow-scale pressure distribution maps," real-time heart rates, and respiratory values into the homepage bed cards. However, user testing immediately proved this wrong: nursing stations monitor up to 40 beds simultaneously. The massive pressure maps and fluctuating physiological data made the screen extremely cluttered, requiring endless scrolling to find the focal point.
Decisive Extreme Subtraction
I tried removing the pressure maps and turning them into bar shapes, but users reported that the blood pressure numbers remained chaotic among dense blocks. I deeply realized that the sole mission of this panel during emergencies is to "accurately locate the problematic bed," not to conduct a diagnosis.

I decisively removed routine physiological data, retaining only 3 absolute pieces of information on the homepage: "Bed No.", "Patient Name", and "Status". This ensures that within 0.1 seconds of an alarm sounding, the medical staff's brains don't need to filter out noise to lock onto the target bed.

Iteration 2: Anti-Fatigue Colorology—Balancing "Alertness" and "Visual Comfort"

Visual Fatigue Caused by High Contrast
Initially, to pursue maximum recognizability, I adopted a highly aggressive color strategy: using thick solid black borders for Focus (selected state) and highly saturated pure red filling the background of alert cards.
Color Tuning with Clinical Empathy
However, I personally simulated the "night shift" scenario for nurses: after exhausting, high-pressure ward rounds, they still have to stare at screens handling paperwork for hours. Large areas of pure red and black would cause severe glare and visual fatigue.

Balancing "alert recognizability" and "reducing visual burden," I readjusted the color scheme. I lowered the background saturation to a soft coral red and brightened the text accordingly. This "gentle yet clear" protective design successfully accommodates both the absolute prominence needed for emergencies and the comfort required for prolonged viewing.
